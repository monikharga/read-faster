candidate Mihiri Gunasekara from QUT's School of Civil and Environmental Engineering, the research brings together four studies examining surface accumulation, pollution hotspots, land-use influences and rainfall-driven microplastic transport in Brisbane catchments. "If we are able to predict where and when microplastics accumulate, local governments and water managers could intervene with street cleaning or stormwater measures." The researchers found that microplastic accumulation slowed after about 10 dry days, which suggested a useful window for tackling the buildup of microplastics before rain washes them into waterways. "Although microplastics may continue to accumulate during longer dry periods, the slowing accumulation suggests that ongoing inputs may increasingly be balanced by processes that remove or redistribute particles," Gunasekara said.

"As the dry period gets longer, gains and losses move toward a balance with new deposits, surface retention, and removal and redistribution through wind or resuspension by pedestrians and vehicles." The researchers first collected dust from asphalt surfaces at QUT's Gardens Point Campus to investigate variations in dust buildup over different numbers of preceding dry days and create a microplastic buildup curve.
